From drone flight to damage description

After a disaster, inspectors walk every damaged site to measure and describe what failed for FEMA Public Assistance. Sortie shows how drone imagery and computer vision could hand them a pre-filled, reviewable draft instead: triage a whole area in minutes, zoom into the facilities that matter, and assemble the evidence behind each line of a Damage Description and Dimensions (DDD).

Step 1

Sweep

One wide-area flight gives every building a damage level on the Joint Damage Scale and marks every blocked or flooded road, so teams know where to go and how to get there.

Real Hurricane Ian labels
Step 2

Site

A detailed flight over a priority facility finds component damage on a 3D model: missing roofing, exposed decking, debris. Each defect keeps its evidence, measurement and uncertainty, and a reviewer confirms it.

Illustrative defects
Step 3

Scope

Confirmed defects become DDD line items. Anything a drone can't see goes on the inspector's field checklist. The inspector verifies, and FEMA's Grants Manager produces the final DDD.

What the Sweep view shows

  • Building footprints and damage levels for part of Fort Myers Beach, FL, labelled by human annotators from drone imagery flown after Hurricane Ian (2022).
  • Road debris, obstruction and flooding labels from the same imagery. Routes avoid impassable segments.
  • Building heights, the staging point and the five public facilities are placed for the demo. Their footprints and damage labels are real; their roles are not.
  • These are human labels, not model output. A model trained on this dataset would produce the same kind of map.
How to use it
  • Drag the 3D map to orbit, scroll to zoom, and click any building for its label.
  • Press Replay sweep flight to watch the drone survey the island.
  • Pick a facility, check its route from staging, then open Review site in 3D to confirm, reject or send defects to the field.
  • Open Scope to see the DDD draft build from your decisions, and copy the inspection fields as CSV.

Data: CRASAR-U-DROIDs, Center for Robot-Assisted Search and Rescue, Texas A&M (Manzini, Murphy et al.), CC BY 4.0, with Microsoft building footprints and OpenStreetMap roads as packaged there. Dataset page. This demo does not determine eligibility, cause or cost, and is not affiliated with FEMA.
